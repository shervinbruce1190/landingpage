Authentication and authorization are basic principles in terms of security, often used together and yet serving a variety of malicious functions:


Certificate of Authenticity
Purpose: Authentication is the process of verifying the identity of a user or device. It assures that a person or company is what they claim to be.

How it really works: It typically involves a aggregate of credentials like:
Something you recognize: Passwords or PINs.
Something you have: Security tokens, clever playing cards, or mobile telephones (for receiving OTPs).
Something you are: Biometric records like fingerprints, facial popularity, or retina scans.
Outcome: Successful authentication grants the person get admission to to the system, however it doesn’t determine what actions they could perform in the machine.
Authorization
Purpose: Authorization is the system of determining what an authenticated consumer is allowed to do. It controls access to sources and moves within a system.
How it works: Authorization commonly includes guidelines and rules that outline:
Access Control Lists (ACLs): Who can get admission to positive statistics or carry out precise movements.
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC): Assigning permissions based totally on person roles inside the employer.
Permissions: Specific rights to view, edit, delete, or create resources.
Outcome: Successful authorization lets in the person to perform unique movements or access particular resources as described by means of their permissions.
Key Differences
Sequence: Authentication comes first; it establishes identity.Empowerment follows; It specifies what that identifier is allowed to do.
Functionality: Authentication is about identity verification, while authorization is ready access management.
Scope: Authentication is associated with user credentials, while authorization is associated with customer authorization and access rights.
Example of scenario
Authentication: Assume you are logged into your email account. You enter your username and password, and also send a verification code to your phone. This process authenticates you, confirming that you are indeed the owner of the account. Upon successful completion of this position, you will receive a certificate

Permissions: Now that you’re logged into your email account you’ll want to do a variety of things—read emails, send messages, or maybe even manage account settings. However, if you’re using a shared or company email system, your ability to perform certain actions may be restricted. For instance, you might not have the authorization to delete emails from a shared inbox or change security settings. These permissions are defined by your role and the system’s policies.

In this case:

Authentication is the process that allowed you to log in by verifying your identity.
The license sets out what you are allowed to do while logged in, such as reading, sending, or viewing emails based on your access rights.
This distinction is important for ensuring that even if someone has access to the system (authentication) they can only do the things they are allowed to do (authorization).
